Correct DNS configuration is the foundation of reliable, trustworthy email. When you send an email from your domain, the receiving mail server performs a series of DNS lookups to verify that your message is legitimate and that it was sent from an authorised server. If these DNS records are missing or misconfigured, your emails may be rejected, sent to spam folders, or your domain may be spoofed by attackers. This guide explains each of the four critical email DNS record types — MX, SPF, DKIM, and DMARC — and how to configure them for your Netstreet Africa domain.

## MX Records — routing incoming email

An **MX (Mail Exchanger) record** tells other mail servers where to deliver email sent to your domain. Without a correctly configured MX record, no-one can send email to `@yourdomain.co.za`.

### What an MX record looks like

```
Type:     MX
Host:     yourdomain.co.za
Value:    mail.yourdomain.co.za
Priority: 10
TTL:      3600
```

- **Host** — your domain name (or `@` as a shorthand in some DNS panels)
- **Value** — the hostname of the mail server that accepts your email
- **Priority** — lower numbers have higher priority; use `10` for a single mail server
- **TTL** — time-to-live in seconds; `3600` (1 hour) is standard

<Note>
  If you are using Netstreet Africa email hosting, the MX record values are
  provided in your Hosting Panel. If you have migrated email to an external
  provider (e.g. Google Workspace or Microsoft 365), those providers will give
  you their own MX record values.
</Note>

## SPF Records — authorising sending servers

An **SPF (Sender Policy Framework) record** is a DNS TXT record that lists the mail servers that are authorised to send email on behalf of your domain. When a receiving server gets a message claiming to be from `@yourdomain.co.za`, it checks the SPF record to confirm that the sending server is on the approved list.

### Why SPF matters

Without an SPF record, spammers can easily send email that appears to come from your domain. This is known as **email spoofing** and can seriously damage your domain's reputation.

### What an SPF record looks like

```
Type:  TXT
Host:  yourdomain.co.za
Value: v=spf1 include:netstreet.co.za ~all
TTL:   3600
```

SPF record syntax explained:

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `v=spf1` | Declares this as an SPF record |
| `include:netstreet.co.za` | Authorises Netstreet Africa's mail servers |
| `mx` | Authorises servers listed in your MX record |
| `a` | Authorises the IP address of your domain's A record |
| `ip4:x.x.x.x` | Explicitly authorises a specific IPv4 address |
| `~all` | Soft fail — mark unauthorised senders as suspicious |
| `-all` | Hard fail — reject unauthorised senders outright |

<Tip>
  Use `~all` (soft fail) while testing, and switch to `-all` (hard fail) once
  you are confident all your legitimate sending servers are included. A
  misconfigured `-all` can cause your own legitimate emails to be rejected.
</Tip>

<Note>
  Netstreet Africa supports SPF records. You can add a custom SPF record to your
  DNS zone via the Hosting Panel. If you need assistance, open a support ticket
  at [my.netstreet.co.za](https://my.netstreet.co.za) and specify the exact
  record you want added.
</Note>

## DKIM — cryptographic email signing

**DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail)** adds a cryptographic digital signature to every outgoing email. The private key lives on the mail server; the corresponding public key is published in a DNS TXT record. When a receiving mail server gets your message, it looks up the public key in DNS and uses it to verify the signature. If the signature is valid, the server knows the message was genuinely sent by your mail server and has not been tampered with in transit.

### What a DKIM DNS record looks like

```
Type:  TXT
Host:  default._domainkey.yourdomain.co.za
Value: v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIGfMA0GCSqGSIb3DQEBAQUAA4GNA...
TTL:   3600
```

- The **Host** follows the format `selector._domainkey.yourdomain.co.za`. The selector is typically `default` or a custom name provided by your email service.
- The **Value** contains your public key. This is generated automatically by your mail server — do not try to create it manually.

## DMARC — policy enforcement and reporting

**DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance)** builds on SPF and DKIM by telling receiving mail servers what to do when an email fails both checks. It also provides a mechanism for receiving daily reports about messages that claim to be from your domain.

DMARC requires both SPF and DKIM to be configured first. It then adds a policy layer:

| DMARC Policy | Effect |
|---|---|
| `p=none` | Monitor only — do not reject or quarantine |
| `p=quarantine` | Send failing messages to the spam folder |
| `p=reject` | Reject failing messages outright |

### What a DMARC record looks like

```
Type:  TXT
Host:  _dmarc.yourdomain.co.za
Value: v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.co.za; pct=100
TTL:   3600
```

DMARC record syntax explained:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `v=DMARC1` | Declares this as a DMARC record |
| `p=quarantine` | Policy: quarantine failing messages |
| `rua=mailto:...` | Send aggregate reports to this email address |
| `ruf=mailto:...` | Send forensic reports to this email address (optional) |
| `pct=100` | Apply the policy to 100% of messages |

### Recommended rollout approach

<Steps>
  <Step title="Start with p=none">
    Deploy a monitoring-only policy first. This lets you receive reports without affecting email delivery:

    ```
    v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.co.za
    ```
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review your DMARC reports">
    After a week or two, review the aggregate reports sent to your reporting address. Identify any legitimate sending sources that are failing DMARC and ensure they have correct SPF/DKIM configuration.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Move to p=quarantine">
    Once you are confident all legitimate senders are covered, change the policy to `p=quarantine`.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Move to p=reject">
    After another period of monitoring, move to `p=reject` for the strongest protection.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Summary: required DNS records for email

| Record | Type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `yourdomain.co.za` | MX | Routes incoming mail to your mail server |
| `yourdomain.co.za` | TXT (SPF) | Authorises which servers may send email for your domain |
| `default._domainkey.yourdomain.co.za` | TXT (DKIM) | Publishes your public key for email signature verification |
| `_dmarc.yourdomain.co.za` | TXT (DMARC) | Defines policy for failed SPF/DKIM checks and reporting |

<Info>
  DNS changes can take up to 24–48 hours to propagate worldwide. After adding or
  updating DNS records, use a tool like
  [MXToolbox](https://mxtoolbox.com) to verify that your records are resolving
  correctly.
</Info>
